Your Victim Impact Statement,
in Your Words

A victim impact statement is your chance to tell the court how the harm has affected your life. Finding the words can be the hardest part, and you shouldn’t have to face a blank page alone. We make the writing easier: you tell us what you want the court to know, and we help you put it in order, in your own words, at your own pace.

  1. We listen. A trauma-informed conversation about how the harm has affected your money, health, safety, family and work. You decide what to share and when.
  2. We help you shape it. We organize what you’ve told us into a clear draft, in your own words.
  3. You revise. You change anything until it sounds like you. Nothing stays in that you don’t agree with.
  4. You approve and sign. The statement is yours, and you are its author.
  5. We help you deliver it. We coordinate with the prosecutor’s victim assistance coordinator on format and deadlines, practice with you, and can sit with you in court if you read it aloud. Court time isn’t part of the $450 flat fee; we quote it in writing first.

Your statement is about your experience. If it mentions dates or dollar amounts, we help you check them against your records. Tell us your sentencing or hearing date when you first contact us, so we can plan around it. Requests about sentencing or restitution go through the prosecutor or your attorney; VAA does not give legal advice. VAA does not provide counseling or therapy.

When the Person Harmed
Is an Older or Disabled Adult

  • The adult is the client. We speak with them directly, or through their lawyer if they have one, and get their agreement. A relative who pays for our work doesn’t become the client or see their information without the adult’s consent.
  • Power of attorney. If the person holding power of attorney may be the one misusing it, we don’t share information with them.
  • Capacity. If there’s a question about the adult’s ability to make decisions, we work through their attorney or court-appointed guardian (holding power of attorney isn’t the same). If they have neither, we don’t start paid work; we point the family to Adult Protective Services, free legal help or an elder-law attorney instead.
  • Reporting. Texas law requires anyone who suspects abuse, neglect or financial exploitation of an adult 65 or older, or of an adult whose disability substantially limits their ability to care for themselves, to report it. Other states have similar laws. If you’re the older adult, a report is about whoever took advantage of you, not about you. We report right away when the law requires. We tell the adult when it’s safe to, and we never tell anyone who may be involved.
  • Open investigations. If Adult Protective Services or the police already have the case, we give the investigator what we have, with the adult’s consent, never contact the person suspected, and leave original documents untouched.
  • Guardianship. When the attorney for a guardianship applicant engages us, we work under that attorney’s direction and deliver our work to them. We can gather records for the application, the inventory and annual accounts.
  • If you’re the family member who called. We talk with you first and agree on next steps before we contact anyone else. If what you tell us means the law requires a report, we make it right away, and we tell you unless that could put someone at risk. We never contact the person you suspect. On the free call, we help you decide who to report to and what records to look for, even if your parent hasn’t agreed to anything yet. Whether you see your parent’s records depends on your parent’s consent. Texas law generally keeps reports to Adult Protective Services confidential, including who made them.

Don’t Have an Attorney Yet?

We can suggest attorneys we know who handle matters like yours, licensed professional counselors (LPCs) if you would like emotional support, and other services your situation calls for. We don’t judge a lawyer’s legal skill, so ask any lawyer about their experience with cases like yours. You can also call the State Bar of Texas Lawyer Referral Service at 800-252-9690, or your own state bar’s referral service. You decide whether to contact anyone, any engagement is directly between you and them, and VAA never pays or receives referral fees.

Already Working with an Attorney?

With your authorization, we coordinate with your attorney and follow the agreed communication plan. Keep contacting your legal team directly for legal advice, case updates and strategy; VAA adds personal and practical support throughout the process.

I Already Have a Free Victim Advocate. Do I Need You?

Maybe not. Advocates through a district attorney’s office, a shelter or a victim services program are free, and they may be all you need, so start there. Private support can add help gathering and organizing your records, one steady person across criminal and civil matters, and support after the case closes. If you work with us, we can work alongside your advocate.

Will My Calls Be Recorded or Shared?

We don’t record our conversations with you unless your attorney asks us to, and we’d tell you and ask your permission first. When you work with us through your attorney, we share updates with your legal team as agreed in writing. If you come to us on your own, we keep what you tell us confidential except where the law requires otherwise. For example, Texas law requires anyone who suspects abuse, neglect or financial exploitation of an adult 65 or older, or of an adult whose disability substantially limits their ability to care for themselves, to report it to Adult Protective Services. Before you share details about something that happened to you, we’ll explain whether it’s something we’d have to report, and to whom. Conversations with VAA are not protected the way conversations with your lawyer are. In practice, that means the other side in a legal matter could, in some cases, ask a court for what you’ve told us. If we ever receive a subpoena or other request about you, we will tell you, and your attorney if you have one, promptly where the law allows. If you’re unsure whether to share something, ask your attorney first.

A Word About Recovery Scams

Be cautious of anyone promising to recover lost money for an upfront fee. We don’t recover money, and we won’t promise to. We never ask for gift cards, cryptocurrency or wire transfers. If you hire us on your own, we never ask you to pay before you receive the work. If someone says they’re from VAA and asks you to pay up front, it isn’t us. We never make sales calls; we call only people who contacted us or whom you asked us to call. If someone else calls saying they’re from VAA, hang up and call or text us at 817-805-2467.

You can report suspected fraud to the FBI’s IC3 ↗ or the Federal Trade Commission ↗.
